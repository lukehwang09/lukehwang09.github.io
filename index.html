Research

Computational Fluid Dynamics, Itai Cohen Lab+

In Professor Itai Cohen's lab, I have worked with computational fluid dynamics and fluid–structure interaction in insect flight. Rather than treating a wing as a stationary lifting surface, these models account for an immersed structure moving through fluid and, where relevant, deforming under aerodynamic and inertial loads. Computational modeling is useful here because the forces are transient and closely tied to the formation and shedding of vortices.

Governing fluid equations

For an incompressible Newtonian fluid, the velocity field \(\mathbf{u}(\mathbf{x},t)\) and pressure \(p(\mathbf{x},t)\) satisfy conservation of momentum and mass. The body-force term can represent forces exchanged with an immersed moving structure:

\[ \rho\left(\frac{\partial\mathbf{u}}{\partial t}+\mathbf{u}\cdot\nabla\mathbf{u}\right)=-\nabla p+\mu\nabla^{2}\mathbf{u}+\mathbf{f},\qquad \nabla\cdot\mathbf{u}=0. \]

The relative importance of inertia and viscosity is captured by the Reynolds number, while the reduced frequency describes the timescale of wing motion relative to flow over a chord length. Both help distinguish flapping-wing problems from steady fixed-wing approximations.

\[ \operatorname{Re}=\frac{\rho Uc}{\mu},\qquad k=\frac{\omega c}{2U}. \]

Immersed-boundary formulation and wing deformation

In an immersed-boundary approach, a Lagrangian description of the wing is coupled to an Eulerian fluid grid. Forces imposed along the wing are spread into the fluid using a regularized delta function; fluid velocities are then interpolated back to material points. In schematic continuous form, the two operations are

\[ \mathbf{f}(\mathbf{x},t)=\int_{\Gamma}\mathbf{F}(s,t)\,\delta_h\!\left(\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{X}(s,t)\right)\,ds, \]
\[ \frac{\partial\mathbf{X}(s,t)}{\partial t}=\int_{\Omega}\mathbf{u}(\mathbf{x},t)\,\delta_h\!\left(\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{X}(s,t)\right)\,d\mathbf{x}. \]

Here \(\mathbf{X}(s,t)\) represents the wing configuration and \(\mathbf{F}(s,t)\) its force density. In a flexible-wing model, the aerodynamic forces affect deformation while deformation changes the flow and the force distribution. The mathematical description therefore requires two-way coupling rather than a fixed geometry with a prescribed flow field.

The instantaneous aerodynamic force and moment can be obtained by integrating surface traction:

\[ \mathbf{F}_{\mathrm{aero}}=\int_{S}\boldsymbol{\sigma}\mathbf{n}\,dS,\qquad \boldsymbol{\sigma}=-p\mathbf{I}+\mu\left(\nabla\mathbf{u}+\nabla\mathbf{u}^{T}\right). \]

These concepts have influenced my interest in morphing-wing aircraft. An adaptive wing introduces a similar design problem on a different scale: its changing geometry alters the flow, while the resulting loads constrain how the wing can deform.

Transmission Electron Microscopy, Judy Cha Lab+

My work in Professor Judy Cha's lab involves atomic-structure modeling and computational transmission electron microscopy, including workflows using abTEM. The central challenge in interpreting atomic-resolution images is that the recorded intensity is not a direct picture of the atoms. It depends on electron scattering, specimen thickness and orientation, the incident probe, lens aberrations, and detector geometry. Simulations help connect a known structural model to the contrast that an electron microscope would produce.

Atomic models and the scattering potential

A simulation begins with atomic coordinates and a periodic simulation cell. The atoms determine an electrostatic potential \(V(x,y,z)\) that acts on the electron wave. For numerical propagation, the specimen is divided into thin slices along the beam direction, with each slice represented by its projected potential:

\[ V_n(x,y)=\int_{z_n}^{z_n+\Delta z}V(x,y,z)\,dz,\qquad T_n(x,y)=\exp\!\left[i\sigma V_n(x,y)\right]. \]

The transmission function \(T_n\) describes the phase shift produced by the slice, with \(\sigma\) the relativistic electron interaction constant. The projected potential depends on atomic species and positions, which is why different projected atomic columns can produce distinct image contrast.

Multislice wave propagation

The multislice algorithm alternates the electron's interaction with a thin slice and its propagation through free space. Starting with an incident wave \(\psi_n\), one step is written as

\[ \psi_{n+1}=\mathcal{F}^{-1}\!\left\{P_{\Delta z}(\mathbf{k})\,\mathcal{F}\!\left[T_n\psi_n\right]\right\}, \]
\[ P_{\Delta z}(\mathbf{k})=\exp\!\left(-i\pi\lambda\Delta z\lvert\mathbf{k}\rvert^2\right). \]

Here \(\mathcal{F}\) denotes the Fourier transform, \(\lambda\) is the relativistic electron wavelength, and \(\Delta z\) is the propagation distance. Repeating this operation through the specimen captures the buildup of dynamical scattering and channeling, which become increasingly important with thickness. Unlike a single projected image, the exit wave depends on the sequence of scattering events through the entire sample.

Discretization choices affect both accuracy and runtime. Pixel sampling determines the accessible spatial frequencies and scattering angles; slice thickness must resolve the changing potential along the beam. Simulations of repeated unit cells therefore require careful attention to lateral extent, propagation grid, and the number of slices.

Probe formation and aberrations

In STEM, a convergent probe is placed at each scan coordinate \(\mathbf{R}\). The reciprocal-space probe is shaped by the aperture and by a phase function describing aberrations such as defocus and spherical aberration:

\[ \Psi_{0}(\mathbf{k};\mathbf{R})=A(\mathbf{k})\exp[-i\chi(\mathbf{k})]\exp[-2\pi i\mathbf{k}\cdot\mathbf{R}]. \]

For an axisymmetric lens, a simplified aberration phase uses the convergence angle \(\alpha\), defocus \(\Delta f\), and spherical aberration coefficient \(C_s\):

\[ \chi(\alpha)=\frac{2\pi}{\lambda}\left(\frac{\Delta f\,\alpha^2}{2}+\frac{C_s\,\alpha^4}{4}\right). \]

The probe diameter and tails control how strongly neighboring atomic columns contribute to a pixel. Consequently, changes in convergence semiangle or aberrations may alter the apparent sharpness of atomic columns even if the atomic arrangement is unchanged.

Detectors, HAADF contrast, and 4D-STEM

After multislice propagation, the exit wave is transformed into a diffraction pattern. An annular detector integrates intensity across a chosen angular interval. In a simplified detector model, the signal at scan position \(\mathbf{R}\) is

\[ I_{\mathrm{ADF}}(\mathbf{R})=\int_{\theta_{\mathrm{in}}\leq\theta\leq\theta_{\mathrm{out}}} \left|\widetilde{\psi}_{\mathrm{exit}}(\mathbf{k};\mathbf{R})\right|^2\,d^2\mathbf{k}. \]

Bright-field and different annular-dark-field detectors measure different portions of the angular distribution and therefore emphasize different scattering mechanisms. High-angle annular dark-field (HAADF) imaging often exhibits approximate atomic-number sensitivity, but quantitative interpretation requires accounting for thickness, thermal scattering, probe conditions, and dynamical effects. A brighter column cannot automatically be interpreted as a heavier atom without these checks.

A pixelated detector retains the complete diffraction pattern for every probe position, producing a four-dimensional dataset \(I(R_x,R_y,k_x,k_y)\). This permits post-processing into multiple virtual detector images, radial integrations, and angularly resolved measurements without rerunning the wave propagation. It also makes the data substantially larger than a conventional ADF image.

\[ I_{\mathrm{4D}}(R_x,R_y,k_x,k_y)=\left|\widetilde{\psi}_{\mathrm{exit}}(k_x,k_y;R_x,R_y)\right|^2. \]

Thickness, convergence, and interpretation

Thickness-series simulations are useful because scattering contrast can change nonlinearly as electrons propagate through successive atomic planes. By repeating the atomic structure along the beam direction and comparing simulated signals at increasing thickness, one can separate purely structural contrast from effects induced by dynamical scattering or probe channeling. An additional complication is thermal motion: the frozen-phonon method averages intensities from ensembles of displaced atomic configurations to approximate thermal diffuse scattering.

For me, the value of computational TEM lies in this connection between atomic models and measurable signals. The process demands both physical understanding and numerical judgment: a simulation can run without error yet still be misleading if its thickness, sampling, angular collection limits, or specimen orientation do not match the question being asked.

Leadership and Projects

Aerial Robotics Club+

I founded the Aerial Robotics Club to create opportunities for students to design, fabricate, and program UAVs. I lead projects spanning CAD, airframe construction, electronics integration, and flight software, and have coordinated collaboration with Cornell's CUAir team.

One of our main projects is a custom VTOL aircraft combining vertical takeoff capability with fixed-wing flight. This has involved mechanical integration and tradeoffs among structural stiffness, aircraft mass, propulsion, and control requirements.

OrchardEye+

Fire blight is a bacterial disease that damages apple and pear trees. Identifying suspicious symptoms across large orchards can be labor-intensive, particularly when growers need to inspect many rows frequently.

Through OrchardEye, we are developing an affordable drone-imaging and machine-learning workflow to flag areas that warrant closer field inspection. Rather than treating aerial imagery as a definitive diagnosis, the intended output is a prioritized inspection map. We are collaborating with local farms, including Indian Creek Farm, to keep the project grounded in growers' practical needs.
